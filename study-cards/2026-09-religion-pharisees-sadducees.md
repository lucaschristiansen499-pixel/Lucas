# Religion — Pharisees, Sadducees, and the Zealots (intro)

## TL;DR
Comparing the major Jewish religious/political groups active during Jesus's ministry — the Pharisees, the Sadducees, and (lecture cuts off mid-introduction) a third group, likely the Zealots — focusing on what each believed and why each clashed with Jesus.

## Terms
- **Pharisees** — A Jewish religious group closely tied to the Law of Moses. Believed in the resurrection of the body and in angels/spiritual beings (this was corrected mid-lecture — see "Watch out for" below). Interpreted scripture more loosely/broadly.
- **Sadducees** — A smaller but very powerful and higher-status group, more like the judicial/governmental branch of Jewish leadership. Denied the resurrection and the existence of angels. Read scripture (the Pentateuch) very literally/strictly, and didn't think the text was specific enough to support resurrection.
- **Sanhedrin ("the Jewish court")** — The Jewish judicial body; the Sadducees held the authority to sentence people through it. The high priest role (the only person allowed into the Holy of Holies) came from the Sadducees.
- **Third group ("the ___," name unclear — see below)** — Described as the "radical group," motivated by scripture's language about Israel as the homeland and a call to "zeal and enthusiasm" to defend God's promised land. Likely the **Zealots**, based on context, but the lecture audio wasn't clear enough to confirm the name.

## Watch out for
- Mid-lecture correction: the teacher initially recapped (from the prior day) that **Pharisees did *not* believe in the resurrection** — then a student's question prompted a correction: it's actually the **Sadducees** who denied resurrection and angels, while the **Pharisees affirmed** both. Use the corrected version.
- Political setup: Rome allowed the Jews limited self-government (hence the Sanhedrin and a nominal "king of the Jews" with little real power), but Rome held final authority and cared mainly about avoiding revolt and enforcing loyalty to the emperor — not about internal religious disputes.
